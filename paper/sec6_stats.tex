\section{Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:stats}

The paired experimental design yields, for each value of $n$ and each trial
$b \in [B]$, a triple $(\hat{p}^{\mathrm{rand}}_b, \hat{p}^{\mathrm{gr}}_b,
\hat{p}^{\mathrm{SA}}_b)$ evaluated against the same $50{,}000$ draws.
The within-trial differences $d_b = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{SA}}_b -
\hat{p}^{\mathrm{rand}}_b$ are i.i.d.\ across trials and are tested by a
two-sided paired one-sample $t$-test of $H_0: E[d_b] = 0$.
Cohen's $d$ effect size \cite{Cohen1988} is defined as
$d_{\mathrm{eff}} = \bar{d}/s_d$, where $\bar{d}$ and $s_d$ are the
sample mean and standard deviation of $\{d_b\}$.

Table~\ref{tab:ttest} reports the results.  Every $n \ge 2$ is
statistically significant at the $0.001$ level; for $2 \le n \le 75$ the
$p$-values are below $10^{-10}$, and at $n = 100$ (where both strategies
are already at $P_{\mathrm{win}} \approx 1$) $p = 5.3 \times 10^{-4}$.
Cohen's $d$ ranges from $1.26$ at $n=2$ to $6.15$ at $n=15$; every
conventional threshold of practical significance (small: 0.2, medium: 0.5,
large: 0.8) is exceeded by an order of magnitude in the useful range of
$n$.

The absolute gain $\bar{d}$ peaks at $n=10$ ($+10.4$ pp) and exceeds six
percentage points throughout $5 \le n \le 20$.  The effect size
$d_{\mathrm{eff}}$ peaks at $n=15$ ($6.15$) and stays above $3$ throughout
$5 \le n \le 50$.  A notable asymmetry is that the trial-to-trial standard
deviation of $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{rand}}$ is five to eight times larger than
that of $\hat{p}^{\mathrm{SA}}$ in the range $3 \le n \le 20$ (and far
larger beyond): the optimised strategy produces both higher expected
probability and far more predictable outcomes, because the SA output
concentrates on the same balanced overlap profiles across trials (for
$n \le 6$, provably the optimal ones), while random collections spread
over the full profile distribution.

Since $d_{\mathrm{eff}} = t_n/\sqrt{B}$, the reported effects would remain
detectable with far fewer trials: at $d_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 4$, five
trials suffice for a conventional $t$-test at the $0.05$ level.  The
paired design is what makes this possible; an unpaired comparison would
mix the Monte Carlo noise of $50{,}000$-draw estimates into every
difference.

For a practical saturation characterisation, define $n^*_\epsilon$ as the
smallest tested $n$ for which $P_{\mathrm{win}}^{\mathrm{SA}}(n) \ge
1-\epsilon$.  From Table~\ref{tab:pwin-full}, $n^*_{0.01} = 25$ and
$n^*_{0.001} = 40$ for the optimised strategy, versus $n^*_{0.01} = 50$
and $n^*_{0.001} = 75$ for the random baseline: optimisation buys the same
near-certainty at roughly half the budget.  The meaningful range for
optimisation is $n \in [2, 40]$, where the absolute gain is at least one
percentage point; within this range, $n \in [5, 20]$ offers the best
return, with gains above six percentage points and $d_{\mathrm{eff}} \ge
3.9$.
